# R01-C-03 — Culvert trash screens block in a storm and flood homes; councils still drive round to look

> Research note: WebFetch is blocked in this environment, so no page was opened in full. Every
> bullet below relies on the title and extract shown in WebSearch results and is marked
> "(search extract)". 8 queries were run (the per-idea budget). Geography is UK unless stated.

## Idea (neutral statement)
- Problem: Debris can block trash screens at culvert inlets during storms. Water then backs up
  into homes and roads. Councils inspect screens on a schedule and after rain; only a minority of
  screens have telemetry.
- Who suffers / who pays: Residents flooded downstream; the council (as screen owner, highway
  authority or Lead Local Flood Authority) pays for inspections, clearance and investigations.
- Sensor concept: Level sensors upstream and downstream of the screen; the difference in level
  (head loss) indicates blockage. LoRaWAN or NB-IoT, solar or battery. Alerts plus an inspection
  and clearance log.

## 4. Compliance, insurance & audit pressure
- Lead Local Flood Authorities investigate significant flooding under Section 19 of the Flood and
  Water Management Act 2010; Herefordshire treats three or more properties flooded internally in
  the same locality as significant and notes there is no national definition (search extract) —
  https://www.herefordshire.gov.uk/downloads/file/27418/section-19-report-ross-on-wye-may-2024-accessible
- Riparian duty: the Brook Road, Tunbridge Wells Section 19 report says a housing association was
  the riparian owner of the culvert opening and screen and has a legal duty to let water pass
  without obstruction (search extract) —
  https://www.kent.gov.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0020/187013/Brook-Road,-Tunbridge-Wells-section-19-flood-investigation.pdf
- Guidance: the EA's 2009 Trash and Security Screen Guide was withdrawn in May 2020 and replaced by
  CIRIA's Culvert, Screen and Outfall Manual (C786F, 2019) plus the EA Blockage Management Guide
  (SC110005, Nov 2019) (search extract) —
  https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5ec7a8c386650c76af0699f0/Withdrawn_-_Trash_and_Security_Screen_Guide_2009_-_report.pdf
  and https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/60378f76d3bf7f03967d33fb/Blockage_management_guide_-_guide.pdf
- Scotland: local authority guidance under the Flood Risk Management (Scotland) Act 2009 covers
  clearance and repair, including screen visit frequency (search extract) —
  https://gov.scot/publications/flood-risk-management-scotland-act-2009-guidance-duties-local-authority/pages/5
- No statute found that requires continuous monitoring of screens; the pressure is duty of care,
  Section 19 scrutiny and reputational risk.

## Researcher observations
- Strong pain evidence: Section 19 reports repeatedly name blocked screens, and Bloxham shows up to
  35 homes flooded by one screen. Southend gives a hard clearance cost.
- The Fife figures (£3.4k + £1.3k/yr) show willingness to pay for a single high-risk screen, and
  Carmarthenshire shows the upstream/downstream method is accepted by councils.
- Competition is forming at the low-cost end (Culvert Sense pilot cohort for LLFAs; cThings
  LoRaWAN), so speed and channel matter.
- Better channels may be hydrometry contractors (Hydro-Logic-type) and councils' existing LoRaWAN
  networks. Riparian owners such as housing associations (Tunbridge Wells case) are another buyer
  for the same problem.
