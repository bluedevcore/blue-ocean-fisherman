# R01-B-08 — Engineered-stone worktop fabricators cutting quartz with the water suppression off

Note on method: WebFetch is blocked in this environment, so no page was fetched. Every bullet below
relies on WebSearch result titles, snippets or the search tool's summary of them, and is marked
"(search extract)". Where the search summary did not make clear which of several listed URLs carried a

## Idea (neutral statement)
- Problem: Engineered (quartz) stone contains a high share of crystalline silica. On bridge saws and hand tools, the wet-cutting water may be switched off, blocked or reduced, and nothing records whether water was flowing during each cut.
- Who suffers / who pays: Stone cutters and polishers (silicosis). Workshop owners pay through enforcement, stop-work orders and claims.
- Sensor concept: An inline flow switch/sensor on each tool's water feed plus a current clamp on the tool or saw. LoRaWAN or LTE-M. Logs the % of cutting time with water flowing, alerts on dry running, and produces a monthly COSHH control-measure report.

## 3. Current workarounds
- HSE position: "no dry cutting of engineered stone". HSE research found dry fabrication typically gives RCS exposure "five to ten times higher than wet methods using equivalent tools". Engineered stone can contain up to 95% crystalline silica (UK, 2026) (search extract) — https://press.hse.gov.uk/?p=8009 ; https://www.ukconstructionmedia.co.uk/news/hse-says-no-dry-cutting-of-engineered-stone-ahead-of-inspection-crackdown/ ; https://www.britsafe.org/safety-management/2026/hse-bans-dry-cutting-of-kitchen-worktops-ahead-of-inspection-drive
- Supplier training: Caesarstone launched the "Master of Stone" online health and safety training for UK fabricators (nine modules, ~1 hour), and says more than 650 UK fabricators received its publications (UK; article date not shown) (search extract) — https://www.stonespecialist.com/news/health-safety-ethical-environmental/caesarstone-launches-master-stone-online-training-centre
- Caesarstone publishes a fabrication good-practice guide via its CS Partners portal (US/Canada) (search extract) — https://mos.caesarstoneus.com/health-and-safety/good-practice-guide/ ; https://mos.caesarstone.ca/health-and-safety/good-practice-guide/
- US OSHA saw fact sheets treat water at the blade as the control: "Water flow rates must be sufficient to minimize the release of visible dust". The summary did not say which fact sheet carries the phrase (US) (search extract) — https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA_FS-3631.pdf ; https://boisestate.edu/oshcon/wp-content/uploads/sites/665/2019/09/OSHA_FS-3633-Walk-Behind-Saws.pdf
- Vendor guidance on bridge-saw water flow conflicts: "1.5 to 3 gallons per minute at the blade" versus "between 3 and 8 gallons per minute of cooling water at the cutting head". The vendor notes that "a saw that appears to be wet-cutting but has significantly reduced actual water delivery at the blade is generating dust that goes unseen" (US, vendor). The summary did not say which of the two pages carries each figure (search extract) — https://dynamicstonetools.com/blogs/news/bridge-saw-water-recycling-systems-slurry-management-guide ; https://dynamicstonetools.com/blogs/news/stone-shop-ventilation-protecting-against-silica-dust-exposure
- No source showed any workshop logging water-on time per cut today. The current control is observation and inspection.

## Researcher observations
- Very strong on c4 and c10 timing: HSE launched a dedicated 2026 engineered-stone campaign (1,000+ visits, stop-work notices, "no dry cutting"), UK case counts are reportedly rising fast (8 → 45), and Australia has banned the material. Pressure is current and rising.
- The sensor directly evidences the HSE's key control (wet methods). The Victorian prosecution shows enforcement can turn on whether wet cutting was used.
- Weak points: small, cost-sensitive buyers, with ~650+ UK fabricators as a rough universe (search extract), and the risk that the UK bans or restricts engineered stone outright, which would shrink the market. Fouling and bypass of flow sensors on slurry-recycling saws is an engineering risk.
- Possible better channel: slab suppliers (Caesarstone, Cosentino) already fund fabricator safety training and face liability arguments (Cosentino's NZ case), so they may subsidise a "verified wet-cutting" kit for their fabricator networks. Kitchen retailers could also require it from subcontracted fabricators.
