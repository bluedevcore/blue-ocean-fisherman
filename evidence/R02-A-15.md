# R02-A-15 — Homecare patients' domestic fridges freeze or warm high-cost biologics and nobody knows until a replacement is ordered

## Idea (neutral statement)
- Problem: Clinical homecare providers deliver refrigerated high-cost medicines (e.g. biologics) to patients' homes. After delivery, storage depends on the patient's domestic fridge, which may run too cold or too warm. Providers and the NHS cannot see post-delivery storage, so excursions surface only when a patient reports a problem or a replacement is requested.
- Who suffers / who pays: Quality / operations director at a homecare medicines provider (buyer); NHS trusts and commissioners (fund medicines); patients; homecare nurses.
- Sensor concept: Small LTE-M fridge puck (no reliance on patient Wi-Fi) shipped with the first delivery, alerting the provider on out-of-range temperature and providing an excursion record to support or refuse replacement.

## 2. Frequency
- Brazil, 81 patients with home-fridge thermometers (Advances in Rheumatology 2020): 67 (82.71%) did not keep home storage correct, with temperatures from −4.4°C to 15.5°C (BR) (search extract) — https://advancesinrheumatology.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s42358-020-00131-x/tables/5 ; https://doaj.org/article/c9bbb725a3654e06a6d4fbe5f01c430e
- Netherlands, 50 patients and 276 injectors fitted with temperature sensors (ACR abstract): "11.6% of the injectors were stored within the recommended temperature range" (NL) (search extract) — https://acrabstracts.org/abstract/the-majority-of-biologic-injectors-are-stored-under-suboptimal-conditions-at-home/
- Utrecht thesis: biologics kept in fridge doors were stored at higher mean temperatures (9.2°C) and were more often outside 2–8°C (NL) (search extract) — https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/371374
- Cuiabá, 60 patients (2019 congress abstract): 39 (65%) stored their biologic outside the recommended range (BR) (search extract) — https://arx.iweventos.com.br/evento/reumato2019/trabalhosaprovados/naintegra/1993
- Scientific Reports (2021) summarises two Spanish studies in which at least half of patients did not keep an acceptable mean temperature during transport and home storage (ES) (search extract) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-85413-0.pdf
- A vendor (SaluTemp, MIT Solve entry) cites Vlieland 2016 as finding that 90% of biologic patients store medication outside the recommended range (vendor citation) (search extract) — https://solve.mit.edu/solutions/69496

## 3. Current workarounds
- Patient leaflets: Kingston and Richmond NHS tells homecare patients to check the fridge regularly and, if it breaks down, to contact the hospital, as "you might still be able to take your medicines (instead of throwing them away)". In a power cut, patients should keep the fridge closed and tell the homecare company to pause deliveries (UK) (search extract) — https://kingstonandrichmond.nhs.uk/patients-and-families/patient-leaflets/reducing-waste-medicines-delivered-homecare
- Sciensus (homecare provider) advises storing medicines in a fridge inside the home, "not an outhouse or garage", set to 2–8°C (UK) (search extract) — https://www.sciensus.com/patients/help/how-do-i-store-my-medication
- Hospital homecare leaflets tell patients to make sure the fridge is "in good working order before the first delivery" and that they have room in it. The summary did not say which of these leaflets carries which line (UK) (search extract) — https://www.nnuh.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Homecare-Medicines-Service20449.1.docx ; https://www.dbth.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/WPR49140-Homecare-PIL-New.pdf ; https://www.uclh.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-information-pages/homecare-delivery-service-multiple-sclerosis
- NHS Lothian's homecare leaflet is adapted from the RPS Handbook Appendix 2c. For nurse-administered infusions, delivery is on the day of administration, which avoids home storage (UK–Scotland) (search extract) — https://policyonline.nhslothian.scot/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/ECC-Homecare-Medicines-Service-for-Infused-Medicines-Pembrolizumab.pdf
- No source quantified provider staff time spent on patient phone check-ins about storage.

- Certification risk: the device would sit in a patient's home and drive decisions on medicine use. Whether it would be a medical device (UKCA/MDR) was not researched. A ±0.5°C DS18B20 is marginal for a 2–8°C band, so a calibrated sensor would be needed (inference from the accuracy figure above).
- Battery and coverage: cellular units quote around 1 year of autonomy, and treatment courses for chronic biologics run for years, so units need swapping or recharging. Signal inside a metal fridge is a known risk (not evidenced here).

## Researcher observations
- Strongest evidence: storage outside 2–8°C at home is very common across several studies, and the homecare market is large (£3.2–£4bn, 500k–705k patients).
- Weakest areas: there is no regulatory duty after delivery and no data on replacement cost or frequency, so c1 and c4 rest on inference. The idea also assumes providers want to know (an excursion record could increase replacement obligations, not reduce them).
- A better buyer may be pharma manufacturers or their patient-support programmes, who fund homecare and care about product integrity and waste. Another option is NHS trust homecare pharmacy teams running waste-reduction work. The incumbent consumer BLE puck (MedAngel, €49) reports to patients, not providers. Cellular B2B is the gap, but a ~$140–$240 retail cellular BOM per patient is heavy unless units are reused.
