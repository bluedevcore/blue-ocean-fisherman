## Idea (neutral statement)
- Problem: Water abstraction licences for spray irrigation usually require a meter, regular recorded readings and returns to the regulator, and some licences require abstraction to reduce or stop when river flow or groundwater level falls below a threshold (hands-off flow/level). Readings are commonly taken by hand. Over-abstraction, missing records, uncalibrated meters and false returns are criminal offences and have been prosecuted.
- Who suffers / who pays: Irrigating farmers and growers (potatoes, field vegetables) who hold licences; the licence holder pays.
- Sensor concept: A pulse or magnetic reader on the existing abstraction meter, plus an optional river-level feed, over LTE-M or LoRaWAN. It logs volume against daily and annual licence limits, alerts before caps are reached and produces a return-ready report.

## 2. Frequency
- The licence sets the recording schedule: "your abstraction licence will tell you how often you need to record the amount of water you abstract", and whether an annual return is required (gov.uk guidance, England) — https://www.gov.uk/guidance/measure-record-and-report-your-water-abstraction (search extract)
- Weekly returns exist: the EA's returns spreadsheet guide notes that where someone must submit "a combination of returns", such as monthly and weekly, the system shows separate lists for each return type (England) — https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7dfc36e5274a2e87daecaf/water_abstraction_spreadsheet_guide.pdf (search extract)
- Nil returns are required where a licence says water use must be reported, even when no water was taken (gov.uk, England) — https://gov.uk/water-management-managing-your-water-abstraction-or-impoundment-licence (search extract)
- Hands-off-flow (HoF) stops recur in dry seasons: the EA said many restrictions came into effect in 2026 due to very low river flows (EA blog, 12 Aug 2026; attribution of this sentence to the blog is inferred from the search summary) — https://environmentagency.blog.gov.uk/2026/08/12/how-the-environment-agency-manages-abstraction-to-protect-the-environment/ (search extract)

## 4. Compliance, insurance & audit pressure
- **Licence + statute:** licences are needed to abstract 20 m³/day or more for irrigation (NFU/EA coverage) — https://farminguk.com/news/farmers-warned-of-crackdown-on-illegal-water-abstraction_66396.html (search extract); in most cases the licence states whether a meter is required (gov.uk) — https://www.gov.uk/guidance/water-abstraction-how-to-make-sure-your-meter-is-accurate (search extract); the licence sets record frequency and returns (gov.uk) — https://www.gov.uk/guidance/measure-record-and-report-your-water-abstraction (search extract)

## Researcher observations
- c4 and c10 look strong: named statute (WRA 1991 ss.57, 201), licence conditions on metering/records, and several recent EA prosecutions specifically for records, meter calibration and over-abstraction, plus an inspection crackdown.
- Fines are modest (£2k–£7k), so c1 depends on the cost of being stopped mid-season or losing licensed volume, which was not evidenced.
- The hardware is cheap (a £105 pulse logger exists off the shelf) — which also means low barriers to entry. The strongest channel is likely **meter suppliers/installers (Bell Flow, DMS) and land agents**, rather than irrigation dealers; Bell Flow already markets automated compliance.
- A possibly stronger niche for the same product: **abstraction licence holders with HoF conditions sharing a catchment** (e.g. abstractor groups/water-trading pilots) where shared live data has value beyond one farm.
