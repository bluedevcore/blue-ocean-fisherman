# R01-B-03 — CNC coolant (metalworking fluid) sumps drift out of spec because weekly COSHH checks are skipped

Note on method: WebFetch is blocked in this environment, so no page was fetched. Every bullet below
relies on WebSearch result titles, snippets or the search tool's summary of them, and is marked
"(search extract)". Where the search summary did not make clear which of several listed URLs carried a
claim, the bullet says so. 8 WebSearch queries were used (budget cap 8).

## Idea (neutral statement)
- Problem: Water-mix metalworking fluids (MWF) in CNC machine sumps need regular checks of concentration, pH and bacterial contamination. When these checks are skipped, fluid quality can drift. That contributes to skin and lung disease, forces sump dump-and-recharge, and leaves no record to show an inspector.
- Who suffers / who pays: CNC operators suffer the health effects. Production, operations or H&S managers at machining businesses pay for fluid, downtime, enforcement and claims.
- Sensor concept: An in-sump probe for conductivity, temperature and liquid level (concentration proxy and top-up need), with optional pH. LoRaWAN/NB-IoT. Produces a weekly per-machine COSHH record and drift alerts.

## 2. Frequency
- HSE's 2023–24 unannounced MWF inspection campaign (Oct 2023–Mar 2024, GB) found that "more than half" of inspections identified failings. The three common failings were no LEV, "failing to regularly conduct fluid quality checks" and failing to do worker health checks (UK) (search extract) — https://press.hse.gov.uk/?p=5731 ; https://www.britsafe.org/safety-management/2024/businesses-are-failing-to-protect-workers-from-dangers-of-metalworking-fluids ; https://myosh.com/news/metalworking-fluids-most-businesses-inspected-not-doing-enough-to-protect-workers
- HSENI ran a focused MWF inspection campaign on manufacturing and engineering companies in October 2018 (Northern Ireland) (search extract) — https://hseni.gov.uk/news/hseni-inspection-campaign-metal-working-fluids-initiative
- Powertrain case numbers: HSE's May 2006 update cited "101 workers ... diagnosed mainly with either occupational asthma or extrinsic allergic alveolitis". A 2006 HSE research report counted 87 workers (10.4% of the workforce) meeting case definitions. The trade press called it the site of the "largest outbreak of work-related disease" (UK) (search extract) — https://am-online.com/news/2006/4/28/longbridge-factory-site-of-largest-outbreak-of-work-related-disease/11708 ; https://www.hse.gov.uk/Research/rrhtm/rr441.htm
- Check cadence: weekly concentration, pH and dip-slide checks per sump, i.e. ~52 records per machine per year (UK guidance; see section 4).

## 8. Buildability (components, BOM, certification)
- Measurement difficulty: refractometer readings with the wrong refractive index factor give "concentration errors of 30–40%". Conductivity is only a proxy, and the blog proposes calibrated baselines with alarm thresholds. The summary did not say which of these two vendor-blog pages carries the error figure (search extract) — https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/machine-coolant-testing-bacteria-ph-tramp-oil-guide ; https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/cnc-coolant-monitoring-sensors-technical-comparison

## Researcher observations
- Strong c4: explicit weekly-check guidance, 5-year record retention, and a recent national HSE campaign that found failure to do fluid quality checks among the three common failings.
- Weak c1: the direct cost of a bad sump (fluid, disposal, labour) looks small in the sources found. The big costs are health claims and enforcement, which are rare per site.
- Buildability risk is material. Commercial systems pull samples to an external refractometer rather than leave a probe in the sump, so a conductivity-proxy probe may struggle with accuracy and fouling.
- Better channel or buyer: MWF suppliers (Fuchs, QH, Oemeta, Master) already send technicians and sell fluid-management contracts. A white-label sensor that lets them cover more sumps between visits, and feeds HSE-ready records, looks more promising than selling direct to job shops.
