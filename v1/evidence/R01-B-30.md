# R01-B-30 — Executors of empty probate homes can't prove the insurer's inspection and drain-down conditions

Note on method: WebFetch is blocked in this environment, so no page was fetched. Every bullet
below relies on WebSearch result titles/snippets/summaries and is marked "(search extract)".
8 WebSearch queries were used (budget cap). Geography is UK (England & Wales) unless stated.

## Idea (neutral statement)
- Problem: A deceased person's home can sit empty for months during probate. Unoccupied-home insurance usually carries conditions such as periodic inspections, with water drained down or heating kept on in winter. If a pipe bursts and the executor cannot show these conditions were met, the claim may be declined.
- Who suffers / who pays: Beneficiaries and executors (including solicitor or professional executors); unoccupied-property insurers and brokers.
- Sensor concept: Rented cellular kit: flow/pulse sensor on the incoming main, pipe/room temperature, door contact and PIR; alerts to a key holder and a weekly evidence pack for the insurer.

## 1. Cost of incident
- Industry-wide escape-of-water (EoW) cost: a Chartered Institute of Loss Adjusters (CILA) fraud bulletin says the ABI confirmed EoW claims "are costing insurers over £2m every day" (UK, 2024) (search extract) — https://cila.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Fraud-Bulletin-EOW-Claims.pdf
- Insurance Times: "Escape of water claims at £2.5m a day", per the ABI (UK) (search extract) — https://www.insurancetimes.co.uk/analysis/escape-of-water-claims-at-25m-a-day/1430915.article
- Zurich broker guide cites "around £2.7 million worth of escape of water losses every single day" (excluding claims below the excess) (UK) (search extract) — https://www.zurich.co.uk/broker/articles/escape-of-water-residential-guide
- Average claim values vary by source: a Which? survey of 1,507 policyholders (June–July 2024) reported an average EoW payout of £1,200 (search extract) — https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/the-most-common-home-insurance-claims-ajOQH1x0W5fK ; Confused.com's own 2021–2024 customer data gives £5,708 per incident (comparison-site data) (search extract) — https://www.confused.com/compare-home-insurance/home-insurance-claims
- No source gave the average value of an EoW claim in an unoccupied/probate home specifically (where damage runs undetected longer). The idea record's "£10–30k+" figure was not verified.
- Personal liability: a UK law firm page on "Devastavit – Executor's Mismanagement Of An Estate" covers claims against executors for loss caused by mismanagement (search extract) — https://www.nelsonslaw.co.uk/devastavit-claim/
- Overseas cases show executors paying personally: a New Zealand firm reports an executor held liable for damage that occurred while estate property was uninsured (NZ) (search extract) — https://www.raineycollins.co.nz/your-resources/articles/executors-responsibilities-keep-insurance-up-to-date-or-pay-the-price-personally ; Wade v Duck, 2018 BCCA 176, held a former personal representative personally liable for deterioration of estate property (Canada) (search extract) — https://www.ahbl.ca/executor-found-liable-for-ducking-her-obligations/

## 4. Compliance, insurance & audit pressure
- Homeprotect executor/probate cover requires an inspection every 14 days with a dated record of each visit (insurer/broker page) (search extract) — https://www.homeprotect.co.uk/?p=7091
- Homeprotect: escape-of-water claims excluded between 1 October and 1 April unless the property is inspected every 30 days (search extract) — https://www.homeprotect.co.uk/?p=1363
- Winter condition: insurers typically accept either a low, steady heating setting or a drained water system (broker) (search extract) — https://www.ukinsurancenet.com/knowledge-base/free-guides-and-checklists/foundations-of-unoccupied-property-insurance/
- Some policies exclude EoW claims if the system was not drained when the condition applied; where there are several executors, one executor refilling a drained system can create liability for all (search extract) — https://todayswillsandprobate.co.uk/?p=57236
- Ageas: a 60-day unoccupancy rule replaces the 30-day one, with cover reduced rather than removed (insurer document) (search extract) — https://www.ageas.co.uk/link/b9c6ac3b392b4caa84efb8fab94e7749.aspx
- Responsibility for compliance sits with the executor; non-compliance can lead to a rejected claim (search extract) — https://lawyer-monthly.com/?p=34053
- Ombudsman: in DRN 3771411 the Ombudsman thought the executors' claim should be considered under the escape-of-water peril (outcome detail from snippet only) (search extract) — https://www.financial-ombudsman.org.uk/decision/DRN-3771411.pdf
- Ombudsman: DRN 5887323 found a decline unreasonable where evidence showed the property was slept in and so did not meet the policy's "unoccupied" definition — i.e. occupancy evidence decides outcomes (search extract) — https://www.financial-ombudsman.org.uk/decision/DRN-5887323.pdf

## Researcher observations
- Strong regulatory-like pressure via insurer conditions: dated inspection records every 14–30 days and drain-down/heating in winter are written into probate policies, and FOS cases turn on evidence of occupancy and inspection — this is close to a "proof" product.
- Weak points: no sourced figure for loss size in probate homes; revenue per estate is short-lived; the substitute (a £15–£25 visit, or £300/month agent package) is cheap.
- Better channel than solicitors may be the specialist probate/unoccupied insurers and brokers (Homeprotect, UK Insurance Net) who could bundle a kit to relax the visit frequency, as Hiscox did with LeakBot for buildings cover.
- The sensor scope could be cut to flow + temperature (drain-down proof and frost) rather than security, which incumbents (Safesite, RemoteZone) already cover.
