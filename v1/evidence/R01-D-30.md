## Idea (neutral statement)
- Problem: Under UK general licences and animal-welfare law, live-catch cage traps (Larsen/corvid traps, grey squirrel and mink cage traps) must be physically inspected at least once every 24 hours. Most visits find an empty trap, and a missed check can be an offence.
- Who suffers / who pays: Gamekeepers and head keepers on shooting estates; forestry and conservation rangers; red-squirrel and mink-control projects (often run by volunteers and paid for by grants). The estate, landowner or project pays.
- Sensor concept: A door-closed (magnetic or tilt) sensor on each trap, sending over LoRaWAN or LTE-M (satellite where there is no coverage). It sends a capture alert and keeps an automatic daily inspection and heartbeat log.

## 1. Cost of incident
- A gamekeeping-trade piece claims manual checking "over a six-month trapping season … can cost over £200 per trap", against "about £50 for a whole year" for automatic trap checking. It bases this on a keeper's full employment cost (housing, vehicle, ATV) divided by the traps checked per day. **Vendor claim:** the author, Dick Bartlett of British Moorlands Ltd, sells automatic trap checkers. The piece ran in a GWCT/NGO magazine, Spring 2014. The PDF host looks like a test mirror and may not resolve (UK) — https://ngo-public-test.aptsolutions.net/media/207/KtBspr14-p48autotrapchecks.pdf (search extract)
- GWCT gives a "typical overall cost of employing a gamekeeper (excluding costs of predator control equipment)" of **£40,000 p.a.** (about £17/hour) in its consultation response on implementing the AIHTS humane trapping standards (UK). The search summary placed this in GWCT's AIHTS consultation response but did not confirm which sub-page; the likely one is the impact-assessment page — https://www.gwct.org.uk/policy/consultation-responses/implementation-of-aihts/impact-assessment (search extract)
- The cost of a missed check is criminal, not insured. An ex-gamekeeper (Alexander Hamilton) was found guilty of causing unnecessary suffering to a magpie left in a Larsen mate trap "in a distressed state due to being confined within the trap" (Scotland). The fine amount was not in the extract — https://endbirdshooting.substack.com/p/ex-gamekeeper-guilty-of-causing-unnecessary (search extract)

## 2. Frequency
- An NE general licence condition, quoted in the BPCA code for GL33 cage traps (England): "When in use, every cage trap used in accordance with this licence must be physically inspected at least once every day at intervals of no more than 24 hours, except where this is not possible because of severe weather conditions." — https://bpca.org.uk/write/MediaUploads/Documents/Codes%20of%20Best%20Practice/BPCA_Code_of_Best_Practice_for_Live_Capture_Trapping_V2_August_2020.pdf (search extract)
- In Scotland: "Check trap(s) at least once every day (at intervals of no more than 24 hours) unless not possible because of unexpected severe weather conditions." — https://www.gwct.org.uk/advisory/guides/larsen-traps-england-scotland/the-legal-position-in-scotland (search extract)
- The Crown Office (COPFS) report of a gamekeeper's prosecution notes that trap licence conditions require checking at least once every 24 hours (Scotland) — https://www.copfs.gov.uk/about-copfs/news/gamekeeper-fined-for-killing-wild-birds (search extract)
- Court material, RSPCA v Shinton [2003] EWHC Admin 1696 (England), quotes the rule that "each Larsen cage-trap which contains a decoy or a live bird must be inspected on at least one occasion in any 24-hour period" — https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2003/1696 (search extract)
- Where red squirrels are present, grey-squirrel traps "must be checked twice daily". A single 24-hour check is cited for grey-only areas (Cumbria Wildlife Trust ranger, UK) — https://squirrelaccord.uk/?p=8967 (search extract)
- In a UK field study of remote monitors on mink traps, using them "reduced the burden of trap visits by 98% compared to the standard protocol of daily checks" (Biological Invasions, A. R. Martin, Univ. of Dundee) — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10530-021-02713-2 (search extract)
- Black Sluice Internal Drainage Board (Lincolnshire, England) says remote-monitored mink traps need checking only about once a month unless a trigger signal arrives, while conventional traps are checked daily — https://blacksluiceidb.gov.uk/news/latest-news/mink-trapping (search extract)

## 3. Current workarounds
- **Daily physical rounds:** a keeper's day mixes trap checks with other estate work and record-keeping (no hours given) — https://www.fieldandmoor.co.uk/blogs/news/a-day-in-the-life-of-a-gamekeeper (search extract)
- The trade article says "most keepers still spend a lot of daily time checking trap status" and that automation would free time for more traps or habitat work (**vendor claim**) — https://ngo-public-test.aptsolutions.net/media/207/KtBspr14-p48autotrapchecks.pdf (search extract)
- Scottish red-squirrel groups recruit **volunteer "grey squirrel dispatchers"** whose role covers "humane dispatch of grey squirrels, liaising with local trap hosts, and re-setting and baiting traps" (Annandale Red Squirrel Group, Scotland) — https://scottishwildlifetrust.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Volunteer-Grey-Squirrel-Dispatcher-Vacancy-Annandale-Red-Squirrel-Group.pdf (search extract)

- In NZ conservation trapping, wireless monitoring is claimed to cut labour costs by 50–80% depending on the control scenario (Forest Bridge Trust, NZ) — https://www.localmatters.co.nz/opinion/the-forest-bridge-trust-smart-traps-smarter-conservation/ (search extract)

## 4. Compliance, insurance & audit pressure
- **England:** general licences are issued under s.16 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Acting outside the licence conditions removes the defence. Under GL38, users need not send records to Natural England but must be able to justify their action if asked — https://gov.uk/government/publications/stoats-licence-to-trap-them-to-conserve-wild-birds-gl38/licence-to-trap-stoats-to-conserve-wild-birds-gl38 (search extract)
- **England, cage traps:** "must be physically inspected at least once every day" (GL33 condition quoted by BPCA) — https://bpca.org.uk/write/MediaUploads/Documents/Codes%20of%20Best%20Practice/BPCA_Code_of_Best_Practice_for_Live_Capture_Trapping_V2_August_2020.pdf (search extract)
- **Scotland:** all trap operators must register in advance with NatureScot, and each trap must carry a tag with the registration number. Registration lasts 5 years — https://gwct.org.uk/advisory/guides/larsen-traps-england-scotland/the-legal-position-in-scotland/ (search extract)
- **Animal Welfare Act 2006:** "Failing to check a trap regularly could be an offence under the Animal Welfare Act 2006" (League Against Cruel Sports factsheet, UK; campaign source) — https://www.league.org.uk/media/filer_public/52/4c/524c69ad-3d7b-46a9-b5cc-780da25eb5bb/traps.pdf (search extract)
- GWCT, the shooting-side research body, states that "if traps and snares are not regularly checked within the legally specified period then the operator is not doing his job correctly and legally" (Aug 2020) — https://www.gwct.org.uk/news/news/2020/august/statement-on-league-against-cruel-sports-report-on-traps-and-snares (search extract)
- **Enforcement:** the Scottish SPCA reminded operators that a crow trap becomes illegal to leave a bird in if it isn't checked at least once every 24 hours — https://raptorpersecutionuk.org/tag/crow-trap/page/5/ (search extract). A Crown Office prosecution of a gamekeeper refers to the 24-hour condition — https://www.copfs.gov.uk/about-copfs/news/gamekeeper-fined-for-killing-wild-birds (search extract). Campaigners allege that at Hoghton Tower (Lancashire) a juvenile crow in a Larsen trap was without water for at least 24 hours and no prosecution followed (campaign source, one-sided) — https://www.wildlifeguardian.co.uk/news/shooting/hoghton-tower-larsen-trap-scandal (search extract)
- **Key legal blocker (UK):** no UK regulator guidance was found that accepts a remote alert in place of the physical daily inspection. The England GL wording says "physically inspected". On a UK stalking forum a poster asked whether the law recognises devices like MinkPolice. A reply warned that the trapper bears the burden of proof: "remote may work great but can you prove you looked?" — https://www.thestalkingdirectory.co.uk/goto/post?id=3454072 (search extract). Shooting Times, on the Western Isles mink project, reports "questions about whether their operation complies with animal welfare legislation, particularly in terms of their ability to report reliably" — https://www.shootinguk.co.uk/news/hebridean-mink-project/ (search extract)
- **Comparable jurisdictions that do allow remote checks (US, not UK):** Virginia allows remote systems to replace the physical visit if the unit reports status at least every 24 h and sends notifications — https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincode/title4/agency15/chapter40/section195/ (search extract). Wisconsin has similar 24-hour status-report conditions and requires a physical check if the system fails — https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/node/119646 (search extract). Michigan permits need an electronic check every 24 h and a physical visit within 24 h of a closure alert — https://www.michigan.gov/documents/dnr/Electronic_Trap_Monitors_680731_7.pdf (search extract). NZ's trap.nz says "do not rely on the notifications for legal coverage" — https://help.trap.nz/books/trapnz-comprehensive-user-guide/page/remote-monitoring-traps-with-sensor-technology (search extract)

## Researcher observations
- The problem is real and mandated daily, which is strong on c2 and c4. But the core value ("stop driving to empty traps") is **legally blocked in England for GL bird traps**: the condition says "physically inspected". A sensor can only speed up dispatch and document checks; it cannot remove the round. This is the critical weakness for c1 and c3.
- The market is already served: PerdixPro LTE-M tags at about €142, plus MinkPolice, Remoti, Keepguard, Vectronic and Celium. The only evident gap is coverage on remote ground (LoRaWAN or satellite) and price.
- **Better niche for the same problem:** invasive-species programmes (mink eradication, grey squirrel control under CWS3 or red-squirrel groups). There, remote monitoring is already practised (a drainage board checks monthly unless triggered; a peer-reviewed study found 98% fewer visits), projects hold grant budgets, and buyers are project managers, not individual keepers. A regulator-endorsed protocol (NatureScot/NE accepting heartbeat plus alert logs) would be the unlock. Lobbying for that is a channel play, not a product play.
